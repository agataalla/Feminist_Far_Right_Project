What 2,000 Telegram Messages Reveal About Collectif Nemesis

The Feminist Right-Wing Group influencing French Politics

By Agatha Allain

You open Instagram.

Collectif Nemesis founder Alice Cordier wanders through a sunny park, playfully chasing a fluffy Pomeranian dog to the tune of California Dreamin'. The camera pans to the group's spokesperson, Yona Faedda, cradling a newborn baby in a white cloth. The scene is peaceful...

And the caption ironically reads: "What enemies of freedom look like. #ExtremeDanger #BeCareful."

Feminist and Far-Right

French flags, angel wings, and floral dresses: Collectif Nemesis’ communications bask in innocence and softness.

Yet its political motives are clear.

Founded in 2019, Collectif Nemesis self-identifies as feminist and far-right. Although the terms might seem contradictory, they make sense to the activists, who believe that foreigners and immigrants are to blame for assaults on women and girls. Even if in 2019, when Collectif Nemesis was created, French nationals were largely over-represented in assaults on women and girls, in 87% of cases (INSEE). Up to this day, the girls’ protest placards read : “Foreign Rapists Out”, “Free us from immigration.”

The collective is part of a growing wave of identitarian, extremist, women’s movements across Europe, alongside their ally Lukreta in Germany and the Pink Ladies in the UK. Their strategy? Invoking gender equality to wrap anti-immigration and Islamophobic agendas in a progressive bow - a phenomenon social scientists call “femonationalism.”

Recently, the group went viral during a visit to far-right political activist Tommy Robinson at a demonstration in London.

Astrid, Alice, and Yona (left to right) staged themselves wearing burqas, asking the crowd to cheer for them to "take it off."

"It was important for us to show that veils do not have their place in European civilisation" Cordier said, "we are alone against the system that wants to destroy our Christian civilisation."

The post gathered over forty thousand likes on Instagram.

In response, Baroness Shaista Gohir, CEO of the Muslim Women's Network UK, criticised the display as a "deliberate humiliation of Muslim women aimed at reducing their religious dress to a source of contempt" for the Middle East Eye.

… but are they doing anything for women ?

It is often said that Telegram can be an interesting place. Collectif Nemesis’ Telegram is no exception to that.

On Instagram, Collectif Nemesis says it is an "association for the protection of women." I wanted to see if their internal communication reflected this promise.

Using the Telegram Developer API, I scrapped and analyzed all messages sent on the Collectif Nemesis Telegram channel, and found that their communication was consistent with hate speech, much less with pro-women’s rights.

Alongside the expected mix of protest photos, call-to-actions, and petitions, you’ll find "Flash Info": chilling messages that read less like activist updates and more like excerpts from a hardboiled French crime thriller.

“‘They are possessed by the devil’: two girls tortured and deprived of food by their mother and her partner, Mohammed, who was a prostitute on homosexual apps.”

“Preys” “barbars” and “executioners” are the protagonists of these messages.

These excerpts contain detailed and, at times graphic, descriptions of assaults on women and girls. Coding with Python’s natural language toolkit library, I found that within the channel’s most recurring topics, Collectif Nemesis uses on average around 30 different words to describe a single assault, spanning from sexual acts, weapons, drugs, and emotional language. In comparison, another feminist channel of a different political side, NousToutes, uses only one or two.

Experts, advocacy groups and journalists have cautioned against using sensationalised assault descriptions. Especially for women who have gone through some form of gender based violence in their life - one in two women in France, according to a 2017 survey. Publishing unformatted, graphic details without warnings can induce in the reader going through a "second assault” and trigger post-traumatic symptoms, for the very audience that Collectif Nemesis seeks to "protect."

Additionally, it’s unclear how Collectif Nemesis offers support for women who have experienced assaults. While the group directs victims to its own activists for legal advice and peer support, their actions tell a different story: according to an investigation released earlier this year by German public broadcaster ARD, a survivor of sexual assault received death threats from Nemesis members after coming forward.

“Tunisian under OQTF”, “Algerian”, “French Cameroonian”… Although both Collectif Nemesis and NousToutes cover major scandals involving French public figures, Collectif Nemesis selectively frames assaults perpetrated by foreign individuals. The posts follow a pattern: a white victim and a foreign perpetrator. Collectif Nemesis reposts from the far-right news aggregator Fdesouche (for “Français de Souche”, “French of ethnic origin”).

Only one feminist channel reposts Collectif Nemesis: the rest are neonazi, identitarian, and ultranationalist

I analysed Collectif Nemesis’ Telegram footprint. Between 2019 and 2023, these groups reposted content from their channel:

- a channel titled "US department of Women Haters",

- the Swiss neonazi group Junge Tat (led by figures such as Manuel Corchia), with which Collectif Nemesis cross-posted “Remigration brings peace” in august last year,

- "Génération Identitaire",dismantled by the government in 2021 for hate speech, and incitation to hatred against religious groups,

- identitarian political figures like Martin Sellner, and Ignaz Bearth,

- channels of “supporters” of Ukrainian ultranationalist factions Azov Regiment,

- conspiracy theory channels such as QAnon or "Dismantling the Cabal",

Ultimately, these groups deploy a mix of martial subcultures, sensationalized media, and radicalizing narrative… one that seems to share little to no common grounds with feminism.

Despite numbering around 300 members, Collectif Nemesis exerts a disproportionate influence in shaping public discourse leading into the 2027 presidential election. When Quentin Deranque, an informal bodyguard for the group, was killed in February 2026 during a violent clash with far-left activists from La Jeune Garde Antifasciste, the event immediately turned into a major political scandal.

Rassemblement National swiftly weaponized the incident to target its left-wing opponent, La France Insoumise. RN leader Jordan Bardella directly assigned political responsibility for Deranque's death to the left-wing party, triggering a wave of fierce accusations and misinformation across the political landscape.

Methodology

Data was collected from two Paris-based activist groups by extracting all Telegram public channel messages for NemesisParis and all TikTok video descriptions for NousToutes. Data was cleaned using pandas and regular expressions before using NLTK and spaCy to run frequency analyses, create word clouds, and plot the top recurring terms. Using the semantic search tool Meaningfully, sentence-level contextual patterns were identified, revealing a trend in NemesisParis's content toward graphic descriptions of assault and perpetrator backgrounds. To test this hypothesis, I constructed an AI-expanded gender-based violence dictionary to quantify co-occurrence frequencies between primary key terms like "women" or "rape" and explicit terms. Full source code, custom French lexicons, and additional documentation are available in the project's GitHub Repository.

Text message artboard was made with Adobe Illustrator. The repost map was made with Flourish using data from TGStat.
